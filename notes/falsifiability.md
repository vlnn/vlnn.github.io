A good hypothesis must make predictions that could, in principle, fail; otherwise it isn't testable.

Keep that in mind when you read "*[Artificial sweeteners may speed declines in memory and thinking](https://www.health.harvard.edu/brain-health/artificial-sweeteners-may-speed-declines-in-memory-and-thinking)*"

* **The headline is unfalsifiable.** "May speed" is compatible with any result, and moreover "speed" is causal language, and that is what readers will remember.
* **The finding is falsifiable; the causal reading is not what was tested.** In \~13K Brazilian adults, higher self-reported intake of six sweeteners was associated with faster cognitive decline over eight years. The association could have been null, and it wasn't, but that tells us little about whether sweeteners *cause* decline.
* **Probabilistic claims don't refute cleanly, so ask about severity instead.** Would this study have found nothing if the effect weren't real? Probably not. Intake was measured once, by questionnaire, at baseline; people who are already sliding metabolically switch to sweeteners; the seventh sweetener, tagatose, showed no association only because almost nobody ate it. The diabetes adjustment and the tertile gradient are real, but they don't close reverse causation or residual confounding.

What would count much better: a pre-registered effect size in a second cohort, repeated exposure measurement, a mechanism behind the specific results. Until then the claim is testable, tested weakly, still open.
