### 1st iteration

Quantum physics is the science of how matter and energy behave at the smallest scales, where things exist as probabilities rather than certainties until measured.

### **2nd iteration**

Quantum physics describes nature at the scale of atoms and subatomic particles. At this scale, objects behave as both waves and particles, can exist in multiple states at once, and only take definite values when measured. These strange rules turn out to be the most precisely tested and successful physical theory ever devised.

### 3rd iteration

Quantum physics emerged in the early twentieth century when phenomena like blackbody radiation and the photoelectric effect proved impossible to explain with classical mechanics, forcing physicists such as Planck, Einstein, Bohr, Heisenberg, and Schrödinger to accept that energy comes in discrete packets called quanta. At its heart lies the principle of superposition: a quantum system is described by a wavefunction that encodes all possible states simultaneously, and this wavefunction evolves deterministically according to the Schrödinger equation until a measurement forces it to yield one definite outcome with a probability given by the Born rule. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle establishes that certain pairs of properties, such as position and momentum, cannot both be known with arbitrary precision — not because our instruments are crude, but because nature itself does not simultaneously define them. Entanglement, which Einstein famously derided as "spooky action at a distance," links the states of separated particles so that measuring one instantly constrains the other, a correlation confirmed by Bell test experiments that ruled out any explanation based on hidden local variables. Today quantum mechanics underpins semiconductors, lasers, MRI machines, atomic clocks, and the emerging fields of quantum computing and cryptography, even as physicists still debate what its formalism ultimately says about the nature of reality.
